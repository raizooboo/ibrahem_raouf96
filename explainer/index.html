How sitagliptin + metformin lowers blood sugar and HbA1c

The tablet combines two medicines that fix different parts of type 2 diabetes. Sitagliptin keeps the gut's after-meal hormones working longer, so the pancreas releases more insulin when glucose is high and less glucagon. Metformin makes the liver produce less glucose, which brings down the fasting level. With glucose lower day and night, less sugar sticks to hemoglobin, and HbA1c falls over the following three months.

1The big picture

Your blood glucose level is a balance. Glucose comes in from food and from the liver, and it leaves the blood when insulin moves it into muscle and fat. In type 2 diabetes the pancreas releases too little insulin at the right moment, keeps releasing glucagon (the hormone that tells the liver to release glucose), and the liver keeps pouring out glucose even when the level is already high.

Each half of the tablet corrects a different part of that picture.

2Sitagliptin keeps the after-meal insulin signal alive

When food reaches the intestine, cells in the gut wall release two incretin hormones, GLP-1 from L-cells and GIP from K-cells. They tell the pancreas to release more insulin, and GLP-1 also tells it to release less glucagon. In healthy people this incretin effect produces roughly half or more of the insulin released after a meal. In type 2 diabetes it is much weaker.

Both hormones are short-lived. An enzyme called DPP-4, found in the blood and on blood-vessel walls, cuts them apart within minutes (about 2 minutes for GLP-1). Sitagliptin blocks DPP-4, so after each meal the levels of active GLP-1 and GIP are 2 to 3 times higher.

Lower glucagon matters as much as higher insulin. Glucagon is the signal that makes the liver release glucose, so less glucagon, together with more insulin reaching the liver, means less glucose output. That is why sitagliptin also trims fasting glucose, although its biggest effect comes after meals.

Why sitagliptin rarely causes low blood sugar

GLP-1 can only amplify an insulin signal that glucose has already started. Inside the beta cell, glucose raises ATP, which closes potassium channels and lets calcium in. Calcium is the trigger that releases insulin. GLP-1 raises cAMP, which strengthens that trigger. When glucose is normal or low there is no trigger to strengthen, so extra GLP-1 adds little insulin. Its glucagon-lowering effect also fades when glucose falls.

3Metformin turns down the liver's glucose output

Overnight and between meals, nearly all the glucose in your blood comes from the liver. In type 2 diabetes the liver overproduces it, largely by making new glucose from lactate, glycerol and amino acids, a process called gluconeogenesis. In a classic study, gluconeogenesis ran at about three times the normal rate in people with type 2 diabetes, and three months of metformin cut it by 36%.

Metformin is carried into liver cells by a transporter called OCT1, where it puts a mild brake on energy production in the mitochondria. Scientists still debate the exact chain of events. Three effects have the strongest support:

  1. 1Glucagon's signal is blunted. The cell's lower energy charge raises AMP, which blocks production of cAMP, the messenger glucagon uses to switch on glucose-making.
  2. 2A key enzyme slows down. AMP directly inhibits fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (FBPase-1), a gatekeeper step of gluconeogenesis.
  3. 3Less raw material is used. A shift in the cell's redox balance, linked to blocking the enzyme mGPD, reduces how much lactate and glycerol become glucose.

Metformin also activates AMPK, an energy sensor that steers the liver away from making fat.

Effects outside the liver

Gut

Metformin reaches much higher levels in the intestinal wall than in blood. It increases glucose use by gut cells, raises GLP-1 release from L-cells, and changes the gut microbiome and bile acids. The gut is also where its stomach side effects come from.

Muscle

Insulin works better (improved insulin sensitivity), so muscle clears more glucose from the blood. Part of this follows from glucose being lower overall.

Appetite and weight

Metformin raises GDF15, a hormone that reduces appetite. Weight usually stays the same or drops slightly. Because it does not make the pancreas release insulin, it rarely causes low blood sugar by itself.

4Why the pair works better than either alone

The two drugs repair different defects, and at one point they directly reinforce each other.

Together they cover both halves of high glucose

HbA1c reflects all the excess glucose across the day: the raised fasting level plus the spikes after meals. Which half matters more depends on how high HbA1c is.

  • Two routes to the liver. Metformin acts inside liver cells. Sitagliptin lowers glucagon and raises the insulin that reaches the liver.
  • Low risk of hypoglycemia. Neither drug forces insulin out when glucose is normal. In the main trial, low-sugar episodes affected 0.5–2.2% of people on active treatment.
  • Weight-neutral, one tablet. Neither drug causes weight gain, and neither changes how the body absorbs or clears the other, which is why they can share a tablet.

5From lower blood sugar to lower HbA1c

Glucose in the blood slowly attaches to hemoglobin inside red blood cells, a process called glycation. Once attached, it stays for the life of the cell. The more glucose around, the more hemoglobin gets coated. HbA1c is the percentage of hemoglobin carrying glucose.

Red blood cells live about 120 days, so HbA1c reflects average glucose over roughly the past three months, weighted toward recent weeks. About half of the result comes from the last 30 days.

Why HbA1c takes about three months to catch up

Blood glucose starts falling within days to weeks of starting the tablet. Red cells made before then still carry their old glucose load, so HbA1c falls only as those cells are replaced. About half of the eventual drop shows up in the first month and nearly all of it by three months, which is why HbA1c is usually rechecked every three months.

6How much does HbA1c fall?

The best direct evidence for this dose comes from a 24-week trial of 1,091 adults with type 2 diabetes that diet and exercise had not controlled. HbA1c at the start was 7.5–11%, 8.8% on average. One group took sitagliptin 50 mg + metformin 1000 mg twice daily, the same as one tablet of this strength twice a day.

Your own result depends on your starting HbA1c (a higher start usually means a bigger drop), how regularly you take the tablet, diet and activity, and what you were taking before.

7Taking it safely

How it is taken

With meals, usually twice daily. Food reduces stomach upset. One 50/1000 tablet twice a day is the maximum, so don't take extra.

Common side effects

Nausea, diarrhea, stomach discomfort or a metallic taste, mostly from metformin and usually easing after the first weeks. Headache, stuffy or runny nose and sore throat with sitagliptin.

Low blood sugar

Uncommon with this tablet alone. More likely if you also take a sulfonylurea (such as gliclazide or glimepiride) or insulin, whose dose your doctor may lower.

Kidneys

Both drugs leave the body through the kidneys. Kidney function (eGFR) is checked before starting and at least yearly. The tablet is not used when eGFR is below 30 and is not started when it is between 30 and 45.

When to pause it

Your doctor may stop it for a short time around X-rays or CT scans that use iodine contrast, before surgery, and during illness with vomiting, diarrhea or dehydration.

Long-term use

Metformin can lower vitamin B12 over years, so levels may be checked, especially with numbness, tingling or tiredness. In the TECOS trial of 14,671 people, sitagliptin did not increase heart attacks, strokes or heart-failure hospital stays.

Get urgent medical help for

  • Severe stomach pain that won't go away, sometimes spreading to the back (possible pancreatitis).
  • Unusual muscle pain, trouble breathing, unusual sleepiness, or feeling cold and dizzy (possible lactic acidosis, which is rare).
  • Swelling of the face, lips or throat, or a severe rash or blisters.

This page explains how the medicine works. It does not replace advice from your doctor or pharmacist, so don't start, stop or change a dose without talking to them. The tablet is not for type 1 diabetes or diabetic ketoacidosis.
